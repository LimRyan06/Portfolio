AlzCare

An accessibility-first mobile application for Alzheimer's patients and caregivers, combining medication support, cognitive activities and adaptive machine learning within one connected system.

Flutter FastAPI Firebase PyTorch GRU Contextual Bandits Healthcare AI
See what I built Project summary in CV View GitHub profile ↗

The problem

Many care tools focus on a single task such as reminders, health logging or cognitive games. They rarely adapt to a patient's changing behaviour or give caregivers a unified view of adherence and cognitive activity.

The solution

AlzCare connects a patient-facing mobile experience with caregiver monitoring, a secure FastAPI and Firebase backend, GRU-based non-adherence risk prediction and contextual-bandit personalisation for cognitive activities.

My role

I designed and implemented the full system: mobile UI, backend services, database integration, machine learning components, testing strategy and caregiver-facing reporting.

Selected AI implementation

These excerpts show how the two adaptive components were implemented rather than only describing them at a high level: the GRU medication-risk model and the contextual-bandit game recommender.

GRU1
GRU model architecture assets/alzcare-gru-architecture.webp

GRU risk-prediction architecture

A PyTorch nn.GRU processes sequential adherence inputs, passes the final hidden representation through a linear layer and applies a sigmoid function to produce a risk score between 0 and 1.

GRU2
GRU training pipeline assets/alzcare-gru-training.webp

Model training pipeline

The training workflow builds sequence tensors, batches them with a DataLoader, optimises binary cross-entropy loss using Adam and saves the learned model weights for the API service.

CB
Contextual-bandit recommender assets/alzcare-contextual-bandit.webp

Adaptive game-difficulty recommender

An epsilon-greedy contextual-bandit service chooses between easy, medium and hard activities, calculates rewards from accuracy and completion time, and persists updated Q-values and selection counts in Firestore.

Competitive programming · Bot strategy · Team collaboration

What this experience was about

Cambridge Battlecode is a six-week programming competition where teams build bots to battle in a turn-based strategy game. Our team, 0_day, competed in the UK Main category and focused on building resource-harvesting, exploration and conveyor-network strategies.

What made the experience especially valuable was the pace of iteration: every improvement had to be tested quickly, observed through match history and visualisers, and then refined again as the competition evolved.

The official Cambridge Battlecode site describes the event as open to teams of 1–4 and includes a non-student team bracket, so the field was not limited only to students. That means we were competing in a broad technical field rather than a student-only classroom setting.

Chapter 01 · Kickoff

Starting the competition and understanding the ladder

We entered Cambridge Battlecode as 0_day and began by familiarising ourselves with the dashboard, ladder and team overview. Early on, the goal was not just to write a bot, but to understand how performance was being tracked and where we stood in the wider field.

  • Set up the team profile and monitored our live rating.
  • Tracked match history to see how quickly changes affected outcomes.
  • Used the ranking screens to keep our development grounded in measurable progress.

Chapter 02 · First working strategy

Build conveyors, collect ore, prove the core idea

Our first workable version focused on a practical objective: build bots that create conveyors to collect ores and route them back to the core. This gave us a stable baseline strategy that started producing results instead of just surviving matches.

  • Implemented a bot that prioritised building conveyor paths for ore collection.
  • Used the visualiser to inspect whether resources were actually being routed efficiently.
  • Validated the idea through successful matches and visible improvement in results.

Chapter 03 · Iteration and momentum

Expand exploration and connect conveyors back to the core

After the first success, we improved the bot by pushing more exploration and connecting conveyor systems more consistently back to the core. This increased our resource flow and helped us build momentum. During this phase, we were winning heavily and reached top 11 in our lobby in the UK Main competition.

  • Improved map exploration to find resources and open better paths.
  • Extended conveyor routing so more of the map fed back into the core.
  • Built confidence through a visible win streak and stronger competitive positioning.

Chapter 04 · Final push and reflection

Staying competitive and narrowly missing the finals

The later stages were about maintaining rank, analysing previous matches and learning from every submission. We remained competitive, stayed strong in the ladder and missed out on the finals only slightly. Even without reaching the final stage, the hackathon was a strong demonstration of rapid iteration, teamwork and practical AI strategy building under pressure.

  • Tracked long-term rating movement and compared it against individual match results.
  • Reviewed bot behaviour from both visualisers and team-overview analytics.
  • Finished with a much stronger system than we started with and a clearer understanding of competitive bot design.
